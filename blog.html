Last Heart Attack Grill Closes: Cardiologists Mourn

To call the Heart Attack Grill a “chain restaurant” would be generous; there were only ever four of them. But for 21 years at least one was open, offering diners the chance to die with a mouthful of fat and a distended belly. That opportunity now has been lost; the last HAG closed, in Las Vegas, on May 18, 2026.

The menu was a smorgasbord of gastronomic horrors, including Flatliner Fries (cooked in pure lard), milkshakes infused with butterfat, and the restaurant’s signature Quadruple Bypass Burger (10,000 calories). Customers who weighed more than 350 pounds got to eat free. Several diners were ambulanced off, and the restaurant’s spokesman, Blair River, died from obesity (he weighed 575 pounds).

Oddly, the risk of eating and collapsing at Heart Attack Grill wasn’t what finally shut the place down. A press release from its owner, “Doctor” Jon Basso, instead blamed “corporate greed” in Las Vegas, which he said was a city that would rather peddle “forty-dollar artisanal avocado toast” than the Grill’s hearty, heavy-duty menu.

Basso was nevertheless optimistic that Heart Attack Grill would rise again in some more honest, high-calorie community, and was proud of what it had accomplished. “The obesity rate has risen from 30 percent since we opened in 2005 to the nearly 45 percent today,” he wrote, “and we pat ourselves on the back for leading the charge.”

Dr Pepper Turns Trash Into Treasure

One way for an owner to make an attraction better (and help pay the bills) is to sell something in its gift shop that can’t be bought anywhere else. Usually it’s a scrap of the attraction itself, such as a baggie of debris from the Twister Museum, or a swatch of Clyde Barrow’s Death Pants from the Bonnie and Clyde Ambush Museum. These are not purchases but investments — like rare art or precious coins — almost certain to rise in value as the supply is finite (the Death Pants swatches eventually ran out).

So we were pleased to learn that the Dr Pepper Museum gift shop now sells shards of glass from old Dr Pepper bottles.

Not just any old bottles, of course.

The museum, in Waco, Texas, occupies the Artesian Manufacturing and Bottling Company building, where Dr Pepper was bottled beginning in 1906. The water for Dr Pepper came from a well drilled down through the first floor. After the well ran dry in the 1920s it became a bottomless trash can, where old, defective Dr Pepper bottles were tossed. Decades of cracked glass piled up. When the capped well was rediscovered during a building renovation, the museum excavated a layer of smashed bottles over 27 feet thick. And the bottom hasn’t been reached yet.

The bottle shards, embossed with old slogans such as “10-2-4” and “Dr Pepper King of Beverages,” are packed like pickles into clear glass jars, large and small size, and sold as “Historic Well Glass” in the museum gift shop for a mark-up appropriate to the contents’ vintage pedigree.

For liability’s sake the museum cautions purchasers not to screw open the lids on the jars. And it covered the now-open well with a lid of clear plexiglass, so that visitors can stand on top and peer into its crunchy depths.

Surprise! You Have Two (or Three) Birthdays

As centennial galas, convergences, and birthday events fill the road trip calendar, excitement is palpable. Route 66 is one century old this year. Humankind agrees it was “born” into the numbered US Highway household in 1926. But what exact date is the big blowout party? That’s complicated, perhaps more than it should be. Are we talking about the “idea” of a Route 66, or the ratification of the designation in the Federal Interstate Highway System? Or when the first sign was hammered into the shoulder?

Human birth dates aren’t set at the moment of conception, or when a baby name is picked.

Do we celebrate landing men on the moon on the day when JFK said “we choose to go to the moon” (Sept. 12, 1962)? Or July 20, 1969, when Nixon rang the Lunar Module to tell Apollo astronauts it was “the most historic telephone call ever made.” Maybe SF writer Jules Verne (1865) and filmmaker Georges Méliès (1902) deserve a slice of the lunar landing cake.
